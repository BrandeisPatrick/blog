Token-Saving Skills Review: Caveman, Ponytail, rtk and Headroom on Opus 5

Four Claude Code token savers and the effort dial, on ten SWE-bench tasks hard enough to fail: 276 isolated Opus 5 runs at extra-high effort. One tool beats turning the dial. Two cost more than running nothing.

Verdict#

Caveman works, and beats the free alternative. −43% cost per run, cheaper on all ten tasks, 24 of 30 solved against the baseline's 25. Turning Claude Code's own effort dial down one notch costs about the same and solves 20.

Ponytail saves, at about the dial's price. −34% cost, 22 of 30 solved: worse than baseline on three tasks, better on none.

rtk and headroom cost more than running nothing — +12% and +21% per run. Both keep their own ledgers of tokens saved; on this traffic neither ledger survived contact with the bill.

On a model that over-thinks, the saver that works is the one that makes it say less. The ones that make it read less had nothing left to cut.

  • Caveman halves the thinking, not just the prose. Reasoning falls 26.3k → 13.2k tokens and turns 47 → 34; with a shorter transcript, replayed context halves too. That is a deeper cut than the dial's high setting makes (15.9k) — and it keeps four more tasks. Told to speak in fragments, the model drops the redundancy and keeps the work. Told to try less, it drops both.
  • Ponytail does write less code — 43 lines added per run against 50, −14%, almost exactly what JetBrains measured — and spends a third less. But it slipped on three tasks and gained on none — one the baseline solves sometimes (0/3 against 1/3), two it never fails. Not significant, and not nothing.
  • rtk had the whole channel and nothing to cut. Opus 5 does 99–100% of its work in the shell: it reads with sed and grep, edits with heredocs, and almost never calls Read or Edit. rtk rewrote some 945 of those commands and its ledger claims 89% saved — against raw output. The agent was already trimming its own output with | tail and -q; what reached the model did not shrink (75.7k characters of tool results per run against 72.4k), and runs got longer: more turns, more test commands, +12% cost.
  • Headroom barely compressed this traffic. Its own ledger: 1.87M of 90.8M tokens removed, 2.1%. In v1, on Opus 4.8 and tasks full of logs and workbooks, it cut replayed context 45%; here the context is source code read in small slices. Meanwhile cache writes rose 37% and turns 13%, and the bill rose with them. Its runs did solve the most — 28 of 30, better on two tasks and worse on none. Checked, because a compressor solving more is odd: all nine disagreeing runs were re-scored from their saved patches in fresh workspaces and every verdict held; the three headroom trajectories share no tool calls; the baseline's misses are real near-misses, and each has a mundane cause: on sphinx-7748 both failing runs wrote the new overload branch without the return annotation, then printed rendered output in which the second signature visibly lacked its -> int — and moved on to editing CHANGES; the one headroom run that made the same slip re-read its own code two calls later and fixed it; on xarray-7229 the failing run chose a defensible other reading of “coordinate attributes should be preserved” (keep the first argument's, not x's), which the issue's own example cannot distinguish and the hidden test does. Five of the six other runs picked x's. Simulated against the pooled per-task rates, an arm this lucky turns up about 1 time in 20 when six arms are tried. So we ran the bigger run, decision rule written down first (PLAN.md §5b): seven fresh trials each of baseline and headroom on the two tasks where they had disagreed. New trials only: headroom 7/14, baseline 11/14 (Fisher p = 0.24); over all ten trials per cell, 6/10 against 6/10 on sphinx-7748 and 7/10 against 8/10 on xarray-7229. The 28/30 was luck.
  • The dial is a blunt instrument. One notch down saves 35% and solves five fewer runs (worse on 5 tasks, better on none); the bottom saves 77% and solves 18 of 30. The first tool worth installing is one that beats that line, and one did.

A task can only separate two agents if one of them might fail it. Set v1's tasks against how often ten public frontier agents on the SWE-bench Verified leaderboard solve them:

public agents = 10 submissions in swe-bench/experiments (OpenHands, Sonar and live-SWE-agent on Opus 4.5; mini-SWE-agent on Opus 4.5, Opus 4.6, Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.2, GLM-5, Kimi K2.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash). The “hard tier” pinned in August and never run had the opposite fault: 2/10, 2/10, 0/10, 0/10 — a floor instead of a ceiling.

So the goal is not harder tasks. It is discriminating ones: tasks the baseline solves sometimes, where a tool that costs capability has a pass rate to move.

v2 design#

One arm per kind of token, no stacks — every effect gets a single cause. All arms run claude-opus-5 at --effort xhigh: the most token-hungry setting is where a saver has the most to cut, and the most to break.

  • Tasks by measured difficulty. Candidates come from public per-task solve rates, then get calibrated here: baseline runs first, and a task is kept only if it passes some but not all of them. The list is frozen before any tool runs.
  • Quality is graded, not one bit: resolved · fraction of target tests passing · regressions · whether tests ran after the last edit · repeated tool calls · patch size.
  • The frontier. Plot cost against pass rate; the effort arms draw the line you get for free. A tool above it earns its install; on it, you could have turned the dial; below it, the dial was better.
  • Power, stated up front: 10 tasks × 3 trials sees drops of roughly 20 points, not 5. Differences are paired by task; intervals are bootstraps clustered by task.

Calibration: 58 baseline runs, ten tasks frozen#

The first pool was chosen where public agents succeed 2–7 times in 10. Opus 5 at extra-high effort solved 18 of 19 on the first try — for this model that pool is the ceiling again. The remaining budget went to a reserve pool that public agents solve at most once in ten; Opus 5 solved ten of those fourteen at least once, eight of them tasks no public agent had solved — among them sphinx-7590, labelled “>4 hours” of human work in SWE-bench.

The surprise is how consistent it is. Of 25 tasks run twice, 17 passed both times, 5 failed both times, and only 3 split. At this effort level a task is mostly either solved or not; there is little luck to average over.

v1 RESULTS · AUGUST 2026 · claude-opus-4-8 · 56 runs

v1 · The matchup#

Caveman makes Claude answer in fragments; headroom compresses what Claude reads. One figure each.

Where it sits: an ordinary plugin — a SessionStart hook injects the ruleset into context, and the model itself changes register.

  • answer text −47% (1.1k → 590 tok/run): explanations and summaries become fragments
  • reasoning −42% (3.8k → 2.2k): the thinking between tool calls shortens with the register
  • tool calls −37% (953 → 605): fewer, tighter calls — the code written is untouched
  • knock-on: cache-read −33% (462k → 308k): a shorter transcript replays less history each turn

Where it sits: a localhost proxy via ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL. Nothing inside Claude Code changes — so behavior doesn't either.

  • what it compresses: the old tool results re-sent every turn — file contents, pytest/log dumps, JSON — each block typed and routed (JSON structurally crushed · code trimmed AST-aware · prose/logs via a small model)
  • cache-read −45% (462k → 254k tok/run): the history is compressed once, and the saving repeats every turn after
  • what it never touches: everything Claude generates — reasoning, answers, tool calls all ≈ unchanged; same fixes, same turn count

After the appeal: zero failures separate the conditions — 10/14 everywhere. The scariest finding reversed on forensics: caveman's speed didn't break the sphinx task, it outran the bytecode cache's one-second mtime resolution and broke our gate. Its −42% reasoning cut shows no measured quality cost in this battery — and this battery could not have shown one: every task sat at the ceiling or the floor (§ why v1 tied). That is the question v2 exists to answer.

Caveats#

  • v1's quality tie is not a finding. Its tasks sat at the ceiling or the floor; its token and cost numbers stand.
  • v1: N=2 — big deltas robust; single-cell effects are flags, not convictions (C3 proved it: the flag was our bug).
  • v2's quality differences are not resolved. 30 runs per arm sees drops of roughly 20 points. Every tool's interval includes zero; only effort-low's excludes it. The cost differences are large and consistent across tasks; the pass-rate differences between tools are leads, not findings.
  • Seven of the ten tasks are ones the baseline always solves, so v2 mostly measures whether a tool breaks something, and can barely see one helping.
  • Dollar figures are the CLI's cost estimate at public API prices; the runs themselves billed a subscription.
  • headroom 0.37.0 and rtk 0.49.0 at their defaults, on one kind of traffic: an agent that lives in the shell and already trims its own output. Log-heavy or JSON-heavy work is a different regime — v1 shows headroom doing well in one.
  • One model per version (Opus 4.8, then Opus 5 at xhigh); each tool's ceiling moves with model and task mix.
  • Native SWE-bench envs (no Docker; official test patches at gate time). v2 envs are ported from the official recipes and verified against the gold patch — still token measurement, not official scores.
  • Harness bugs caught and fixed — v1: per-block stream events; a test-path false-flag; a stale-bytecode gate (C2 re-gated 0/8 → 8/8; C3 caveman 0/2 → 2/2). v2: § leaks closed.
